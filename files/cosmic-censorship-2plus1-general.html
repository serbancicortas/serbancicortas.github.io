Weak Cosmic Censorship in 2+1 dimensions

Penrose's Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture [Pen69] states that, for generic initial data, singularities are hidden inside black holes. This paper establishes the conjecture for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with a negative cosmological constant.

An essential step of the proof is establishing the presence of a mass gap. In particular, this implies that all naked singularities have infinite blueshift, which represents the main instability mechanism.

1.Gravity in 2+1 dimensions

The goal of general relativity is to understand gravity, which is given through the geometry of spacetime. We consider triples \((M,g,\phi)\), where:

  • \(M\) is a manifold of dimension 2+1 or 3+1;
  • \(g\) is a Lorentzian metric with signature \((-,+,\dots,+)\);
  • \(\phi:M\to\mathbb R\) is a scalar field, which models matter in the spacetime.
The signature of \(g\) encodes the fact that locally there is one time direction and the other directions correspond to space. At each point in the spacetime, the vectors \(X\) with \(g(X,X)=0\) form a light cone. One of the assumptions of the theory is that light travels along light cones and massive observers travel on curves that stay inside of the light cones, which encodes the fact that nothing can travel faster than the speed of light.

To study gravity, we consider solutions of the Einstein-scalar field system

\[\begin{cases}\mathrm{Ric}_{\mu\nu}[g]-\tfrac12R[g]\,g_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}[\phi],\\[3pt] T_{\mu\nu}[\phi]=\nabla_\mu\phi\,\nabla_\nu\phi-\tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}\,g(\nabla\phi,\nabla\phi),\\[3pt] \square_g\phi=0.\end{cases}\]

The first line is the Einstein equations, expressed in terms of the curvature of the metric \(g\). On the right hand side of the Einstein equations we have \(T_{\mu\nu}[\phi]\), the energy-momentum tensor of the scalar field. To get a closed system of equations, we add the corresponding equation of motion for matter, which is the wave equation on the curved spacetime \((M,g)\). The resulting system is hyperbolic, and it can be written as a system of nonlinear wave equations in suitable coordinates.

As a simplification, we restrict to symmetric solutions. In 2+1 dimensions, we work in circular symmetry, and in 3+1 dimensions, spherical symmetry. In both cases the metric has the form

\[g=\bar g+r^2\,d\sigma^2,\]

where \(\bar g\) is the metric on the quotient 1+1 Lorentzian manifold, \(d\sigma^2\) is the round metric on \(S^1\) or \(S^2\), and \(r\) is the radius of the spheres. The axis of symmetry is \(\{r=0\}\).

To justify studying the Einstein-scalar field system, we note that matter is needed for dynamics: in vacuum and the symmetry class above, the solutions are rigid (and time independent). The simplest matter model that does not form singularities in the absence of gravity and breaks this rigidity is the scalar field (note that electromagnetism does not break the rigidity, while fluids can form singularities on their own).

The constant \(\Lambda\) represents the cosmological constant (attributed to dark energy). In our universe, \(\Lambda\) is positive, since the expansion of the universe is accelerating. However, \(\Lambda\) is so small that it only matters on the scale of the whole universe, and it is negligible for the formation of black holes. Thus, in 3+1 dimensions, we simply consider \(\Lambda=0\). On the other hand, in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda=0\), no singularities form dynamically, so we take \(\Lambda<0\) instead. We remark that in 2+1 dimensions this is the most elementary model that allows for the dynamical formation of singularities [PC00, JGC15].

2.Global dynamics

The Einstein-scalar field system is hyperbolic, so we study the global behavior of solutions arising from data at an initial time. Note that gravity is an attractive force, while waves want to disperse. There are three possible outcomes in global dynamics:

  1. Non-collapse. No singularities form and the solution exists for all time.
  2. Black hole. A black hole region develops in evolution, defined by the fact that gravity is so strong in this region that nothing can escape it (this is represented using light cones in Fig. 2). Moreover, a singular hypersurface forms inside the black hole, and anything that enters the black hole region encounters this singularity in finite time.
  3. Naked singularity. A curvature singularity \(b\) forms, but there is no black hole region. The singularity is visible to far-away observers. Note that by finite speed of propagation, the equations cannot be solved beyond the future characteristic cone arising from \(b\) (see the dashed cone in Fig. 2).

Perhaps surprisingly, naked singularities are more pathological than black holes. In a black hole spacetime, observers who never enter the black hole region exist for all time, so general relativity only breaks down as a physical theory inside the black hole region. For naked singularities, on the other hand, all observers stop existing within finite time: they reach the future cone of \(b\) and see the singularity.

3.The Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture

One of the main open problems in general relativity is the Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture, proposed by Penrose in 1969 [Pen69]:

Conjecture (Penrose ’69) · Weak Cosmic Censorship

For generic initial data, naked singularities do not form. That is to say, singularities are generically hidden inside black holes and invisible to far-away observers.

As explained in the previous section, the conjecture is motivated by the fact that naked singularities would threaten the predictability of general relativity as a physical theory. In its full generality, the conjecture is completely open. We will discuss the resolution of the conjecture for the model problems in symmetry introduced above.

We first consider the model problem of the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 3+1 dimensions, introduced by Christodoulou [Chr71]. In [Chr94], Christodoulou constructed examples of naked singularity spacetimes, which show that the genericity condition in the formulation of the conjecture is necessary. The examples in [Chr94] are self-similar, and they arise from \(C^{1,\alpha}\) initial data. In the monumental work [Chr99a], Christodoulou proved that all naked singularities are in fact unstable to black hole formation. Thus, naked singularity spacetimes are non-generic in a suitable sense, which proves a version of the conjecture for this model. The instability mechanism required working in a low-regularity topology, so the question of Weak Cosmic Censorship in a smoother topology remains open even in this setting.

We now consider the model problem of the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda<0\). We provide a resolution of the Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture in this setting, for initial data of arbitrary smoothness:

Theorem 1.1 · Weak Cosmic Censorship

Consider the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda<0\). For any \(k\ge2\), naked singularities do not occur for generic \(C^k\) initial data.

In particular, naked singularity spacetimes are non-generic and are unstable to black hole formation (Fig. 1).

4.Idea of the proof

The proof has three main steps: a description of singularities, a criterion for black hole formation, and an instability argument.

4.1.Description of singularities

An essential quantity for the description of singularities is the Hawking mass \(m\). This is a quasi-local quantity, defined on each circle of symmetry by

\[m=-g(\nabla r,\nabla r)-\Lambda r^2+1.\]

It measures the energy of the scalar field in the disk bounded by the circle,

\[m\approx\int_0^r r'\,(\partial\phi)^2\,dr',\qquad\text{and}\qquad m=0\ \text{ when }r=0.\]

The system of equations implies that \(m\) satisfies an evolution equation as well. Moreover, the Hawking mass has monotonicity properties, outside of black hole regions.

A key part of our proof is establishing the presence of a mass gap:

Similar statements hold for scaling-critical problems: no singularities form if the energy is below the energy of the ground state. The Einstein-scalar field system with \(\Lambda<0\) has no scaling symmetry, but morally, near \(r=0\), the cosmological constant is lower order.

Theorem 1.2 · Mass gap

No singularities form in the domain of dependence of a disk bounded by a circle with Hawking mass strictly less than 1.

Proof idea

For the proof of Theorem 1.2, the bound on \(m\) first gives quantitative control of the geometry. A non-concentration estimate then shows that the scalar field cannot blow up at a faster than self-similar rate, so \(r\partial_r\phi\) is bounded. This improves by a small power of \(r\): \(r^{1-\rho}\partial_r\phi\) is bounded for some small \(\rho>0\). These bounds propagate the local well-posedness norm from the initial data, so no singularities form.

The main use of the mass gap is the following description of singularities on the axis \(\{r=0\}\):

Corollary 1.1

Let \(b\) be a singularity on the axis of symmetry, and let \(\mathcal C\) be its past light cone. Then, approaching \(b\) along \(\mathcal C\), \(r\searrow0\) and \(m\searrow1\).

4.2.A criterion for black hole formation

In order to prove instability to black hole formation, we first need to establish an effective criterion for black hole formation. The main idea is that if an annular region contains too much energy, relative to the size of the region, then a black hole forms in evolution. This is inspired by Christodoulou's work in 3+1 dimensions [Chr91].

Theorem 1.3 · Black hole formation

On an initial data hypersurface, consider two circles of radii \(r_1<r_2\) and Hawking masses \(m_1,m_2\), and let \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) be their ingoing light cones. Assume that \(C_1\) ends at a singularity \(b\) on the axis of symmetry. The annulus between the two circles has relative width \(\delta_0\) and energy content \(\eta_0\):

\[\delta_0=\frac{r_2}{r_1}-1,\qquad \eta_0=\frac{m_2-m_1}{\lvert\Lambda\rvert\,r_2^2}.\]

If the annulus is thin enough and its energy content is larger than its width, \(\eta_0>\delta_0\), then a black hole forms in evolution.

Following Christodoulou [Chr99a], the criterion is used in two ways:

  • After a suitable perturbation of a naked singularity spacetime, it shows that a black hole forms.
  • Since naked singularity spacetimes do not form black holes, the contrapositive of the criterion gives bounds for the energy on small scales.

4.3.The instability argument

We finally use the two ingredients outlined above to prove the instability of naked singularity spacetimes to black hole formation. Consider any naked singularity spacetime. Along the past light cone \(\mathcal C\) of the singularity \(b\), Corollary 1.1 implies that \(m\searrow1\) and \(r\searrow0\). We explain how this creates a linear instability for the wave equation.

Let \(L\) and \(\underline L\) be the outgoing and ingoing characteristic directions. We note that, at top order, \(\square_g\sim\underline L L\). We consider the following renormalized outgoing derivative of the scalar field:

\[\theta=\sqrt r\,\frac{L\phi}{Lr}.\]

We note that \(\theta\) is invariant to rescaling \(L\), and that \(\sqrt r\) is a natural scale for the wave equation in 2+1 dimensions. The wave equation implies that \(\theta\) satisfies the following equation in the ingoing direction:

\[\underline L\theta=\frac{2\lvert\Lambda\rvert r}{\lvert\Lambda\rvert r^2+1-m}\,\theta+\dots,\]

where we prove that the contribution of the remaining terms is of lower order.

The blueshift \(\mathbf b\) is the integrating factor of this equation: along the ingoing direction, \(\theta\) is multiplied by \(e^{\mathbf b}\), so a perturbation traveling toward \(b\) along \(\mathcal C\) is amplified by the factor \(e^{\mathbf b}\). Since \(m\searrow1\), we get that:

\[\mathbf b\ \ge\ 2\log\frac{r_0}{r},\]

so the singularity has infinite blueshift.

For the naked singularity spacetime itself, everything is finely tuned, and the instability is not activated. Now perturb the initial data by an \(\varepsilon\)-small amount, outside \(\mathcal C\), so the past of \(b\) is unchanged. The main role of the blueshift is to amplify this perturbation as it travels toward \(b\). Recall that the contrapositive of Theorem 1.3 gives bounds for the energy of the background naked singularity spacetime. This is used in an essential way to show that the blueshift instability survives in the nonlinear argument, so the energy of the perturbed spacetime grows.

At this point, it would be naive to expect that this linear instability can be traced all the way to the formation of a black hole, which is a nonlinear process. However, thanks to our black hole formation criterion, it suffices to show that we can control the perturbed solution up to a time where Theorem 1.3 applies, see Fig. 5. Here, we have too much energy in a small enough annulus, so a black hole later forms in evolution.

A comment on regularity. The higher the regularity of the perturbation at \(\mathcal C\), the smaller the perturbation is near \(\mathcal C\). In our case, the different scaling of the problem and the quantitative lower bound \(\mathbf b\ge2\log(r_0/r)\) give quantitative control of the strength of the linear instability, which allows us to use perturbations in any \(C^k\). In Christodoulou's case, there is no quantitative lower bound for the blueshift, so he needs very rough perturbations.

5.Upcoming work

We briefly outline a future direction considered in an upcoming work with Igor Rodnianski, motivated by the physics literature. Numerical relativity works on critical collapse suggest the following picture. Schematically, take an initial data profile \(\Phi\) and an amplitude \(p>0\), and evolve the data \(p\cdot\Phi\):

  1. For \(p\ll1\), the initial data is small, so the solution does not collapse.
  2. For \(p\gg1\), the initial data is very concentrated, so a black hole forms in evolution.
  3. By an intermediate value property, one expects to have a phase transition between the two behaviors. Numerically, it is expected that there exists some \(p^*\) for which the solution forms a naked singularity.

Remarkably, this behavior is universal: it holds for every profile \(\Phi\). It was discovered by Choptuik in 3+1 dimensions [Cho93], see also the review [GMG07], and it was also seen in 2+1 dimensions by Pretorius–Choptuik [PC00] and Jałmużna–Gundlach–Chmaj [JGC15].

This suggests the conjectural picture of the infinite-dimensional moduli space of spacetimes in Fig. 6, where each point represents a solution of the Einstein-scalar field system. The threshold between non-collapsing spacetimes and black hole spacetimes is a codimension-1 set consisting entirely of naked singularity spacetimes. There could also exist naked singularities off the threshold, surrounded by black hole spacetimes.

A more general question. Understanding the phase transition between two behaviors (in our case non-collapse and black hole formation) is a more general question that can be studied outside of general relativity. For semilinear equations, such as the nonlinear wave or Klein–Gordon equations, solitons, or collapsing solitons, can sit on the threshold between dispersion and blow-up [NS11a, NS11b, KNS15]. An essential difference is that in our problem there are no solitons and no conserved energy.

Very little is known rigorously regarding the conjectural picture outlined in Fig. 6. For the Einstein-scalar field models that we introduced above:

  • In symmetry, by [Chr99a] and Theorem 1.1, naked singularity spacetimes are non-generic and are unstable to black hole formation.
  • In 3+1 dimensions, there are examples of naked singularity spacetimes, [Chr94] in spherical symmetry and [RSR23, SR22] for the Einstein vacuum equations. All the known examples arise from low-regularity initial data, and they are not proved to be on the threshold. Moreover, the conjectural threshold examples are difficult to construct, because the numerics suggest they are discretely self-similar [Cho93, CK26].
For the Einstein-dust system (Christodoulou [Chr84]) and the Einstein–Euler system (Guo, Hadžić and Jang [GHJ23]), there are naked singularities arising from smooth data, but these matter models are prone to singularity formation even in the absence of gravity.

In upcoming work with Igor Rodnianski [CR26], we give the first rigorous construction of naked singularities on the threshold of gravitational collapse: (locally) naked singularities arising in (local) critical collapse.

Theorem (Cicortas–Rodnianski) · Threshold examples

There exists a 2-parameter family of \(C^3\) initial data \(\Phi^n_\lambda\), for integers \(n\ge4\) and \(\lambda\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\), such that:

  • for \(\lambda<0\), no singularities form;
  • for \(\lambda=0\), the solution is a (locally) naked singularity and the case \((\lambda,n)=(0,4)\) corresponds to the numerical examples of [PC00, JGC15];
  • for \(\lambda>0\), a black hole forms (locally).
For technical reasons pertaining to the structure of the boundary at infinity due to the negative cosmological constant, the initial data for the examples in the theorem is prescribed in a bounded set. Thus, this result is local in nature, as suggested by our informal terminology of (local) naked singularities and black holes. For a precise mathematical formulation of this result, see the version for readers familiar with general relativity.

We note that unlike all previous naked singularity examples for scalar field models, our examples arise from high-regularity data, as the numerics predict. Moreover, we expect that one can prove that the initial data in our examples are not only \(C^3\), but actually \(C^\infty\).

Additionally, the numerical relativity works [PC00, JGC15] only construct the corresponding \(n=4\) solution, as this acts as an attractor on the threshold. However, we construct a whole family of threshold solutions, indexed by \(n\), for all \(n\ge4\).

Finally, to explain the connection between the parameter \(n\) and the solutions, we note that for \(\lambda=0\) the blueshift has the exact rate

\[\mathbf b=\Big(3-\frac1{2n}\Big)\log\frac{r_0}{r}+O(1),\]

which should be compared with the general lower bound \(2\log(r_0/r)\) of Section 4.3.
