Weak Cosmic Censorship in 2+1 dimensions

Penrose's Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture [Pen69] states that, for generic initial data, singularities are hidden inside black holes. This paper establishes the conjecture for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with a negative cosmological constant.

An essential step of the proof is establishing the presence of a mass gap. In particular, this implies that all naked singularities have infinite blueshift, which represents the main instability mechanism.

1.Gravity in 2+1 dimensions

The goal of general relativity is to understand gravity, which is given through the geometry of spacetime. We consider triples \((M,g,\phi)\), where:

  • \(M\) is a manifold of dimension 2+1 or 3+1;
  • \(g\) is a Lorentzian metric with signature \((-,+,\dots,+)\);
  • \(\phi:M\to\mathbb R\) is a scalar field, which models matter in the spacetime.
The signature of \(g\) encodes the fact that locally there is one time direction and the other directions correspond to space. At each point in the spacetime, the vectors \(X\) with \(g(X,X)=0\) form a light cone. One of the assumptions of the theory is that light travels along light cones and massive observers travel on curves that stay inside of the light cones, which encodes the fact that nothing can travel faster than the speed of light.

To study gravity, we consider solutions of the Einstein-scalar field system

\[\begin{cases}\mathrm{Ric}_{\mu\nu}[g]-\tfrac12R[g]\,g_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}[\phi],\\[3pt] T_{\mu\nu}[\phi]=\nabla_\mu\phi\,\nabla_\nu\phi-\tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}\,g(\nabla\phi,\nabla\phi),\\[3pt] \square_g\phi=0.\end{cases}\]

The first line is the Einstein equations, expressed in terms of the curvature of the metric \(g\). On the right hand side of the Einstein equations we have \(T_{\mu\nu}[\phi]\), the energy-momentum tensor of the scalar field. To get a closed system of equations, we add the corresponding equation of motion for matter, which is the wave equation on the curved spacetime \((M,g)\). The resulting system is hyperbolic, and it can be written as a system of nonlinear wave equations in suitable coordinates.

As a simplification, we restrict to symmetric solutions. In 2+1 dimensions, we work in circular symmetry, and in 3+1 dimensions, spherical symmetry. In both cases the metric has the form

\[g=\bar g+r^2\,d\sigma^2,\]

where \(\bar g\) is the metric on the quotient 1+1 Lorentzian manifold, \(d\sigma^2\) is the round metric on \(S^1\) or \(S^2\), and \(r\) is the radius of the spheres. The axis of symmetry is \(\{r=0\}\).

To justify studying the Einstein-scalar field system, we note that matter is needed for dynamics: in vacuum and the symmetry class above, the solutions are rigid (and time independent). The simplest matter model that does not form singularities in the absence of gravity and breaks this rigidity is the scalar field (note that electromagnetism does not break the rigidity, while fluids can form singularities on their own).

The constant \(\Lambda\) represents the cosmological constant (attributed to dark energy). In our universe, \(\Lambda\) is positive, since the expansion of the universe is accelerating. However, \(\Lambda\) is so small that it only matters on the scale of the whole universe, and it is negligible for the formation of black holes. Thus, in 3+1 dimensions, we simply consider \(\Lambda=0\). On the other hand, in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda=0\), no singularities form dynamically, so we take \(\Lambda<0\) instead. We remark that in 2+1 dimensions this is the most elementary model that allows for the dynamical formation of singularities [PC00, JGC15].

2.Global dynamics

The Einstein-scalar field system is hyperbolic, so we study the global behavior of solutions arising from data at an initial time. Note that gravity is an attractive force, while waves want to disperse. There are three possible outcomes in global dynamics:

  1. Non-collapse. No singularities form and the solution exists for all time.
  2. Black hole. A black hole region develops in evolution, defined by the fact that gravity is so strong in this region that nothing can escape it (this is represented using light cones in Fig. 2). Moreover, a singular hypersurface forms inside the black hole, and anything that enters the black hole region encounters this singularity in finite time.
  3. Naked singularity. A singularity \(b\) forms, but there is no black hole region. The singularity is visible to far-away observers.

In a black hole spacetime, observers who never enter the black hole region exist for all time, so general relativity only breaks down as a physical theory inside the black hole region. For naked singularities, on the other hand, all observers eventually see the singularity and stop existing. This is pathological.

3.Weak Cosmic Censorship

One of the main open problems in general relativity is the Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture, proposed by Penrose in 1969 [Pen69]. It states that, for generic initial data, naked singularities do not form. That is to say, singularities are generically hidden inside black holes and invisible to far-away observers. The conjecture is motivated by the fact that naked singularities would threaten the predictability of general relativity as a physical theory.

Christodoulou proved a suitable formulation of the conjecture for the model problem of the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 3+1 dimensions [Chr99a]. Explicit naked singularity spacetimes can be constructed: Christodoulou's examples [Chr94] are self-similar, and their initial data are \(C^{1,\alpha}\). However, all naked singularities are in fact unstable to black hole formation, and the set of naked singularity spacetimes is non-generic in a suitable sense. The instability mechanism required working in a low-regularity topology, like \(C^1\). Thus, the question of Weak Cosmic Censorship in a smoother topology remains open even in this setting.

This paper initiates the mathematical study of gravitational collapse for the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda<0\), and proves Weak Cosmic Censorship in a topology of arbitrary smoothness: in \(C^k\), for any \(k\ge2\).

4.The main result

Theorem 1.1 · Weak Cosmic Censorship

Consider the circularly symmetric Einstein-scalar field system in 2+1 dimensions with \(\Lambda<0\). For any \(k\ge2\), naked singularities do not occur for generic \(C^k\) initial data.

In particular, naked singularity spacetimes are non-generic and are unstable to black hole formation.

The paper chooses a more elementary notion of genericity than Christodoulou [Chr99a]: naked singularity data lie in the complement of an open and dense set, rather than having positive codimension. The proof shows that naked singularities are unstable to black hole formation: after an arbitrarily small perturbation of the initial data, a black hole forms instead (Fig. 1).

5.Idea of the proof

The proof has three steps: a description of singularities, a criterion for black hole formation, and an instability.

5.1.Description of singularities

The Hawking mass \(m\) is a quasi-local quantity, defined on each circle of symmetry by

\[m=-g(\nabla r,\nabla r)-\Lambda r^2+1.\]

It measures the energy of the scalar field in the disk bounded by the circle,

\[m\approx\int_0^r r'\,(\partial\phi)^2\,dr',\]

so \(m=0\) at the center \(r=0\). More precisely, \(m\) is non-negative, and it vanishes on a circle exactly when there is no matter in the domain of dependence of the disk bounded by that circle: the region of spacetime determined by the initial data on the disk (Fig. 3). Outside of black holes, the Hawking mass has monotonicity properties.

Theorem 1.2 · Mass gap

No singularities form in the domain of dependence of a disk bounded by a circle with Hawking mass strictly less than 1.

Proof idea

For the proof of Theorem 1.2, the bound on \(m\) first gives quantitative control of the geometry. A non-concentration estimate then shows that the scalar field cannot blow up at a faster than self-similar rate, so \(r\partial_r\phi\) is bounded. This improves by a small power of \(r\): \(r^{1-\rho}\partial_r\phi\) is bounded for some small \(\rho>0\). These bounds propagate the local well-posedness norm from the initial data, so no singularities form.

Similar statements hold for scaling-critical problems: no singularities form if the energy is below the energy of the ground state. The Einstein-scalar field system with \(\Lambda<0\) has no scaling symmetry, but morally, near \(r=0\), the cosmological constant is lower order.

The main use of the mass gap is the following description of first singularities at the center:

Corollary 1.1

Let \(b\) be a first singularity at the center, and let \(\mathcal C\) be its past light cone. Then, approaching \(b\) along \(\mathcal C\), \(r\searrow0\) and \(m\searrow1\).

So the energy is 1, but \(r=0\).

Globally, the Hawking mass is non-decreasing away from the center, and it tends to the Bondi mass \(M\) far away. As a corollary, we obtain the mass gap in terms of the Bondi mass:

Corollary 1.2

Solutions with Bondi mass less than 1 do not form singularities. In particular, solutions with small initial data are non-collapsing.

This proves a conjecture of Bizoń and Jałmużna [BJ13], who gave numerical evidence for very small Bondi mass. It marks a sharp contrast with 3+1 dimensions and \(\Lambda<0\), where arbitrarily small initial data can form black holes, see [DH06, BR11, Mos20, Mos23].

5.2.A criterion for black hole formation

If there is too much energy in an annular region, relative to the size of the region, then a black hole forms. This is inspired by Christodoulou's work in 3+1 dimensions [Chr91].

Theorem 1.3 · Formation of trapped surfaces

Consider two circles of radii \(r_1<r_2\) in the initial data, with Hawking masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). Let \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) be their ingoing light cones, and assume that \(r\to0\) and \(m\ge1\) along \(C_1\). Set

\[\delta_0=\frac{r_2}{r_1}-1,\qquad \eta_0=\frac{m_2-m_1}{\lvert\Lambda\rvert\,r_2^2}.\]

If \(\delta_0>0\) is small enough and \(\eta_0>\delta_0\), then a marginally trapped surface forms on \(C_2\).

Proof idea

The proof adapts Christodoulou's argument [Chr91] to 2+1 dimensions: the monotonicity properties of the system give a differential inequality for the energy content of the annulus between \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), as it evolves along \(C_2\).

Here \(\delta_0\) is the relative width of the annulus and \(\eta_0\) is its energy content. A circle is trapped exactly when \(m>1+\lvert\Lambda\rvert r^2\): its energy is too large for its size. It is marginally trapped when equality holds. Trapped surfaces lie inside black holes.

Following Christodoulou [Chr99a], the criterion is used in two ways. Directly: after a perturbation, it shows that a black hole forms. And through its contrapositive: a naked singularity has no trapped surfaces near it, so the criterion gives bounds for the energy on small scales.

5.3.The instability

Along the past light cone \(\mathcal C\) of a first singularity, \(m\searrow1\) and \(r\searrow0\). This creates a linear instability for the wave equation. Let \(L\) and \(\underline L\) be the outgoing and ingoing light-like directions. The renormalized outgoing derivative of the scalar field

\[\theta=\sqrt r\,\frac{L\phi}{Lr}\]

satisfies, in the ingoing direction,

\[\underline L\theta=\frac{2\lvert\Lambda\rvert r}{\lvert\Lambda\rvert r^2+1-m}\,\theta+\dots\]

Near \(\mathcal C\), the mass gap gives \(m\ge1\), and the absence of trapped surfaces gives \(m\le1+\lvert\Lambda\rvert r^2\). So the coefficient is at least \(2/r\), which blows up at \(b\). A perturbation traveling toward \(b\) along \(\mathcal C\) is amplified by the factor \(e^{\mathbf b}\), where the blueshift satisfies

\[\mathbf b\ \ge\ 2\log\frac{r_0}{r},\]

and \(r_0\) is the radius of \(\mathcal C\) at the initial time. So all first singularities at the center have infinite blueshift, whether or not they are naked (Fig. 5).

For the naked singularity itself, everything is arranged just right, and the instability is not activated. Now perturb the initial data by an \(\varepsilon\)-small amount, outside \(\mathcal C\), so that the causal past of \(b\) is unchanged. The main role of the blueshift is to amplify this perturbation as it travels toward \(b\). Since the contrapositive of the criterion gives bounds for the background spacetime, we can control the perturbed solution and apply the black hole formation criterion. So the perturbed spacetime contains a sequence of trapped surfaces converging to \(b\), and its singularity is spacelike-only (Fig. 1).

A comment on regularity. The higher the regularity of the perturbation at \(\mathcal C\), the smaller the perturbation is near \(\mathcal C\). In our case, the different scaling of the problem and the quantitative lower bound \(\mathbf b\ge2\log(r_0/r)\) give quantitative control of the strength of the linear instability, which allows us to use perturbations in any \(C^k\). In Christodoulou's case only a soft statement is available, so he needs very rough perturbations.

6.Upcoming work

Constructing examples of naked singularities plays a fundamental role in understanding their general properties and their instability. Moreover, the existence of such examples justifies the need to include genericity conditions in any formulation of the Weak Cosmic Censorship conjecture.

Numerical relativity works on critical collapse suggest the following picture. Take an initial data profile \(\Phi\) and an amplitude \(p>0\), and evolve the data \(p\cdot\Phi\):

  1. for \(p\ll1\), the solution does not collapse;
  2. for \(p=p^*\), the solution is expected to form a naked singularity;
  3. for \(p\gg1\), a black hole forms.

This behavior is universal: it holds for every profile \(\Phi\). It was discovered by Choptuik in 3+1 dimensions [Cho93], see also the review [GMG07], and it is also seen in 2+1 dimensions by Pretorius–Choptuik [PC00] and Jałmużna–Gundlach–Chmaj [JGC15].

This suggests the conjectural picture of the moduli space in Fig. 6. In this infinite-dimensional space, the threshold between non-collapsing spacetimes and black hole spacetimes is a codimension-1 set of naked singularity spacetimes, consistent with Theorem 1.1. The theorem also covers naked singularities off the threshold, if they exist, showing they are surrounded by black hole spacetimes.

A more general question. Two behaviors, non-collapse and black hole formation, with a threshold between them made of naked singularities: this question is not specific to general relativity. For semilinear equations such as the nonlinear wave and Klein–Gordon equations, solitons, or collapsing solitons, can sit on the threshold between dispersion and blow-up. The differences with our problem: there are no solitons and no conserved energy, so one cannot look at the energy level of the ground state.

What is known about naked singularities:

  • They are non-generic, since they are unstable to black hole formation ✓. Are they also unstable to non-collapse? This is harder.
  • There are examples, by Christodoulou [Chr94] for the spherically symmetric Einstein-scalar field system and by Rodnianski and Shlapentokh-Rothman [RSR23, SR22] for the Einstein vacuum equations ✓. They arise from low-regularity initial data, and they are not proved to be on the threshold. Are there examples on the threshold, arising from smooth data?
For the Einstein-dust system (Christodoulou [Chr84]) and the Einstein–Euler system (Guo, Hadžić and Jang [GHJ23]), there are naked singularities arising from smooth data, but these matter models are prone to singularity formation even in the absence of gravity.

In 3+1 dimensions, threshold examples are difficult to construct, because the numerical critical solution is discretely self-similar. In upcoming work with Igor Rodnianski [CR26], we give the first rigorous construction of naked singularities on the threshold of gravitational collapse: locally naked singularities arising in (local) critical collapse.

Theorem (Cicortas–Rodnianski) · Threshold examples

There exists a 2-parameter family of \(C^3\) initial data \(\Phi^q_\lambda\), for integers \(q\ge4\) and \(\lambda\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\), such that:

  • for \(\lambda<0\), no singularities form;
  • for \(\lambda=0\), the solution is a locally naked singularity with no trapped surfaces, and the case \((\lambda,q)=(0,4)\) corresponds to the numerical examples of [PC00, JGC15];
  • for \(\lambda>0\), trapped surfaces form.

The data are on a finite light cone, so this is a local version of critical collapse. The examples form a tower indexed by \(q\), and in the numerics the \(q=4\) solution is the attractor on the threshold. Unlike all previous naked singularity examples for scalar field models, our examples arise from high-regularity data, as the numerics predict.

For \(\lambda=0\) the blueshift has the exact rate

\[\mathbf b=\Big(3-\frac1{2q}\Big)\log\frac{r_0}{r}+O(1),\]

which should be compared with the general lower bound \(2\log(r_0/r)\) of Section 5.3.
